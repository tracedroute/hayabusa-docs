1) What Hayabusa Is

Hayabusa is designed to be a command center for infrastructure. Whether you are an individual or an organization, Hayabusa is built to cater to both. Create or configure routers, switches, virtual machines, physical servers, containers, applications, and more. Communicate with devices and preserve their configurations as text files over the internet — then monitor security and performance afterward.

With security, simplicity, and education in mind, Hayabusa aims to simplify your infrastructure and teach along the way. It may look like a program with many features to learn, but it is built on commonly used open-source tools, with documentation here to help you start quickly. In fact, there may be features in Hayabusa that you never need to use — and that is by design.

Important distinction: this /docs page is the product manual (concepts and workflows). API route browsing lives on /api. Deep architecture notes for operators also live in the repo under docs/ (for example Image Nest and ZTP/controller architecture).

2) Mental Model

Hayabusa can be simplified to a few layers:

  • Application: the program itself — UI, APIs, Ansible/OpenTofu, dashboards, and helpers doing the work.
  • Network access: the physical means of connecting — cables, NICs, Ethernet ports, radios.
  • Transport: protocols that move data. The two major ones are TCP and UDP, each suited to different programs and reliability needs.
  • Internet: connecting one host to another with IP addressing. When hosts connect across the world, that is the internet as we know it.

You will use all of these in one way or another. Network access, transport, and internet all involve data moving from one device to another. Those three layers form your connection to Hayabusa and Hayabusa’s ability to talk to your computers, routers, switches, and controllers. The application layer does the work before communicating out through the others.

In basic terms, this is how all parts of the platform communicate. Even advanced areas — security monitoring, telemetry, drone control, SCADA, and pentesting — combine these same layers.

When something feels confusing: ask which layer failed first — application logic, reachability (network access), protocol/session (transport), or addressing/routing (internet). That narrows root cause quickly.

3) First-Time Onboarding

Hayabusa Core

Hayabusa Core is the publicly accessible portion of Hayabusa — the brain and middleman for nearly every action. Anyone can sign in and view their networks and hosts, or those of an organization they belong to. Without Core there is no development, security, or other major function.

If you are reading this, you have likely already signed in. Hayabusa currently supports Google, Slack, Discord, and Microsoft logins. That is intentional: it serves users from many platforms and avoids unnecessary risk and liability from storing password-based user credentials on our side.

Walkthrough: map → DevOps → Fleet console

  1. Click Foundry in the top navigation, then configure virtual workstations. That opens the Workstations page — a map of your infrastructure. On a Hayabusa instance on your LAN, discovery can automatically map computers, routers, and switches on the local network.
  2. Open the left side navigation (available on most pages) and click DevOps. That takes you toward infrastructure development and the Fleet page — open Fleet.
  3. On Fleet, the left column lists identified hosts. For first login that may look sparse; the important surface for most users is the console in the center.

The console is organized by use-case categories. Its heart is Ansible and OpenTofu — two major open-source DevOps tools:

  • OpenTofu — declarative infrastructure: “you must look like this; if apply cannot succeed, roll back.”
  • Ansible — procedural configuration: “you should do/have/include this; do not roll everything back on a single failure.”

You write text files that define how infrastructure is created and modified. Those programs are explained further below; for onboarding, know that this is where you define intent before changes run.

To the right, another organization of your networks appears — useful when a controller spans multiple separated networks. You can list hosts by network, zoom the map, and see last-known OpenTofu configuration where available.

Below, a draggable panel embeds the Workstations map so you can watch infrastructure as you edit. In some cases workspace edits show up in near real time.

Essentials: understanding Ansible and OpenTofu through Hayabusa is the backbone of nearly everything else. Use the AI chat assistant to help draft playbooks — for speed and fewer errors — then keep reading the sections that match your role.
Also know: site LAN DHCP/PXE and Image Nest live on a Hayabusa Controller on your network. Core orchestrates claim, GitOps, and OpenTofu/Ansible after LAN Approve. You do not need a controller for the first Core walkthrough above, but you will for ZTP and Nest.

4) Workstations

Open Foundry → configure virtual workstations. Workstations is a clearer, less-cluttered view of mapped infrastructure — a bird’s-eye map for telemetry, planning, and specialized views without the full Fleet console chrome.

During onboarding you saw Fleet as the DevOps command surface. Workstations is the companion map: same world of hosts, focused on visibility and modes rather than every craft action at once.

How to use it effectively

  • Treat the map/diagram as operational context, not the sole source of truth.
  • Confirm host identity with multiple fields (IP, hostname, type, ownership).
  • Refresh host data before sensitive actions.

The telemetry drawer supports per-host or per-link troubleshooting: freshness, RTT/loss/jitter, top talkers, and threshold alerts. Set thresholds per environment (lab, staging, production) rather than one global default.

Path Trace and Baseline Compare help rapid triage: trace checks likely forwarding path; baseline compare shows topology drift in the current window. Baseline compare is in-memory for speed; durable history can be added later if compliance needs it.

4.1) Views Menu, Telemetry, and Map Modes

Click Views (top-left of the map) to switch modes. The telemetry pill stays centered so health stays visible. Available views include: underlay, overlay, observability, diagram, robotics viewer, NVIDIA ISAAC, disasters, weather, SCADA, and air traffic. That list is long on purpose — enterprise teams split roles, and you may never need every view.

Diagram

Diagram is a planning canvas for current infrastructure and future changes with others. Add candidate servers, networks, and more; export and later import XML when you need to save work. If it looks familiar, you have likely used draw.io / diagrams.net — this uses that open-source lineage. Either tool works for planning and visualization.

Overlay

Overlay shows how regional hub connections relate to the central command path — the control plane for sites and branches. In an overlay network you reach infrastructure through a control node (the “puppet master”). On https://hayabusa.tracedroute.net you are on the central command server. That sits upstream of a regional hub or self-hosted Core/controller on a LAN, which in turn sits upstream of the devices you create and configure.

Hierarchy (top → bottom): central server → regional hub / self-hosted instance → devices under management.

Underlay

Underlay is the view of links between switches, routers, and hosts on a LAN. Use it to troubleshoot performance — for example, a PC with buffering while streaming: compare bandwidth or errors on that host’s switch link versus peers, or spot a misconfigured switch.

Observability (map view)

Shows graphs of metrics scraped from infrastructure once collectors and dashboards are in place (often with InfluxDB/Grafana). You typically install or enable scrape/agent pieces as you configure hosts — see Grafana / Prometheus / InfluxDB below.

Robotics Viewer

Uses MAVLink, MAVROS, MAVSDK, and ROS2 for robotics control and programming. Enter vehicle and camera IPs where applicable, plan flights, and watch vehicles on the map. Map controls support pan-to-drone, destinations, waypoints, and toggles for air vehicles, fleets, simulation, and saved routes. Deep detail is later in this manual under SCADA / PLC / ROS2.

Other views (quick map)

  • World Map / geographic context — distributed fleet awareness.
  • SCADA — industrial HMI/controller context (FUXA).
  • NVIDIA ISAAC — remote connector to your own GPU Isaac deployment.
  • Disasters / weather / air traffic — situational layers for operators who need them.

Recommended operator flow

  1. Start in World Map or Diagram to find the host or system.
  2. Use Overlay for application/control-path impact.
  3. Use Underlay when loss, jitter, or L2/L3 adjacency needs explaining.
  4. Open Observability, Robotics, SCADA, or ISAAC only when that domain is the task.
Scope: Workstations respects user and organization context. Confirm the selected organization before provisioning, file, PLC, or console actions.

4.2) Ansible & OpenTofu Workspace

The Ansible & OpenTofu workspace is the tenant-scoped infrastructure-as-code area from Workstations and Fleet. Personal and organization workspaces are separated; organization write access depends on membership and permissions.

Recall the onboarding distinction: OpenTofu is strongly declarative (“must”); Ansible is procedural (“should”). Both are edited as text under your workspace and run through Hayabusa’s approved workflows — ideally drafted with the AI assistant, then reviewed by you.

Console View

Command-oriented: edit files as text, run approved Ansible/OpenTofu workflows, sync OpenTofu state into inventory, and review output. Best for repeatable operations and exact commands.

Graphical View

A FileZilla-style manager for the same workspace — same tenant-scoped APIs as the console:

  • Path bar — current path and quick navigation.
  • Left tree — folders such as ansible/, OpenTofu/, ztp/, bare-metal-ztp/, and openPLC/.
  • Right list — name, type, size, modified time.
  • Preview/editor — inspect and edit text files in-browser.
  • Transfer area — upload/move results and recent status.

Graphical file actions

  • Create folders and files; preview/edit/save text; rename; delete (with write access).
  • Upload by dragging from the desktop; move by dragging onto another folder.

Security model

Workspace APIs validate paths server-side. Absolute paths, traversal, NUL bytes, and symlink escapes are rejected. Uploads are size-limited and written only under the tenant root. Read-only org members can inspect but not mutate.

Relevant APIs include /api/devops-iac/ls, /api/devops-iac/file, /api/devops-iac/mkdir, /api/devops-iac/mv, /api/devops-iac/rm, /api/devops-iac/upload, /api/devops-iac/run, and OpenTofu state sync endpoints.

Operational warning: graphical actions are real workspace mutations. Confirm user/org context before uploads, moves, deletes, or OpenTofu sync.

4.3) OpenPLC Workspace Directory

Each personal and organization workspace includes an openPLC/ tree — a first-class home for PLC projects beside Ansible and OpenTofu. Use it to keep IEC programs, libraries, and generated artifacts under the same tenant scope as your IaC.

Directory layout

  • openPLC/programs/ — IEC 61131-3 projects, Structured Text, ladder/exports, controller sources.
  • openPLC/generated/ — generated exports and build artifacts.
  • openPLC/libraries/ — reusable function blocks and support libraries.
  • openPLC/README.md — tenant-local instructions seeded by Hayabusa.

OpenPLC Editor in the container

OpenPLC Editor is installed under /opt/openplc-editor with launcher /usr/local/bin/openplc-editor; MatIEC is under /opt/openplc-editor/matiec. Edit in Graphical View, then use console/container workflows to open, generate, or export as your environment requires.

Tenant separation

openPLC/ lives inside the resolved workspace root — personal and organization trees do not mix unless you deliberately copy files. Pair with the PLC Viewer (§12) for live reads/writes; keep write approval discipline the same as any other guarded control action.

5) Fleet

Fleet is the multi-host operations surface — answering “which systems?” rather than “this one system.” Use it for rollout groups, online/offline posture across many nodes, inventory anomalies, Incoming/ZTP claim, and the central Ansible/OpenTofu console from onboarding.

Layout

  • Left column — discovered, claimed, or pending hosts (and Incoming when a controller is connected).
  • Center console — categorized craft workspace (Ansible, OpenTofu, ZTP trees, and related tools).
  • Right / map — network-oriented layout, zoom, and last-known OpenTofu configuration where available.

Best use cases

  • Segmenting rollout groups and comparing posture across many nodes.
  • Claiming Incoming devices after controller discovery / ZTP seek.
  • Running approved Ansible/OpenTofu jobs against scoped targets after LAN Approve when a controller holds secrets/media.
Relationship to Workstations: Workstations is the clearer map-first view; Fleet is the craft and inventory command surface. Use both — map for orientation, Fleet for action.

5.1) "Incoming"

Zero-touch provisioning allows a view of hosts "calling out" via certain ports and protocols for their first-time configuration. This allows users to see the hosts calling out, recognize which vendor and model the instance is, and provide it a "recipe" or first-time configuration if it's not already applied.

How to use it

  1. Open Fleet and select the target host.
  2. Open the host console action.
  3. Use Apache Incoming for the remote session.
  4. Switch to PLC Viewer when you need controller protocol work.

6) Networking

Networking is where overlay, tunnel, and tenant isolation are monitored and controlled — the transport and internet layers from the mental model (§2), made operable in the UI.

When diagnosing incidents, prioritize: identity scope → tunnel status → route/selector correctness → host reachability and latency. That order prevents false conclusions from stale or out-of-scope sessions.

Subsections in this category

  • 6.1 WireGuard + Headscale — mesh overlay.
  • 6.2 IPsec — classic/interop tunnels.
  • 6.3 Tenant VPC / Subnet Allocation — private CIDR ownership.

6.1) WireGuard + Headscale

WireGuard provides encrypted transport. Headscale provides self-hosted coordination and identity for mesh networks. Together they support operator-managed overlay connectivity between Core, regional hubs/controllers, and managed devices.

Interpret overlay status with underlay evidence. A tunnel can be “up” while service quality degrades due to jitter or loss beneath it — use map overlay health coloring and link telemetry together before changing peer config.

Operator checklist

  1. Check server/client (or Headscale) status before troubleshooting endpoints.
  2. Confirm tenant / namespace assignment matches the intended organization or user scope.
  3. Verify expected peers appear in host/peer lists.
  4. If quality is bad but the tunnel is up, switch to Underlay (Workstations) and Path Trace before rotating keys.
Mental model: WireGuard+Headscale sit in the transport/internet layers — they move application traffic securely; they do not replace correct addressing or L2/L3 health on the underlay.

6.2) IPsec

IPsec supports tunnel scenarios that need selector-based or interoperability-oriented connectivity (site-to-site, partner networks). It often complements mesh overlay workflows rather than replacing them.

Validate three dimensions independently: IKE/session establishment, selector correctness, and application reachability. Successful IKE does not guarantee selector accuracy; selector accuracy does not guarantee firewall allowance.

Safety note: broad selectors or overlapping CIDRs can create hard-to-debug traffic leaks. Keep selectors explicit and owned. Document who owns each tunnel and what CIDRs it carries.

When to prefer IPsec vs WireGuard mesh

  • IPsec — peer requires classic IPsec/IKEv2, strict selectors, or existing enterprise VPN standards.
  • WireGuard + Headscale — operator-managed mesh, modern clients, simpler key/identity coordination inside Hayabusa.

6.3) Tenant VPC / Subnet Allocation

Hayabusa can allocate tenant private address realms from a configured pool and persist assignments. That creates deterministic ownership of private CIDR space per user or organization so overlays and tunnels do not accidentally collide.

How to operate safely

  • Validate proposed CIDRs before assignment (size, RFC1918 fit, no overlap).
  • Avoid overlap with existing tenant realms and with underlay LAN ranges you still need to reach.
  • Document ownership of each assigned block (org/user, purpose, date).
  • After change, verify routing/overlay peers still reach expected hosts.
Tip: treat tenant allocation as control-plane state. Changing a live tenant CIDR can strand tunnels — plan a migration window.

7) Zero Touch Provisioning (ZTP)

ZTP automates first contact so blank or factory devices enter service with a consistent baseline — DHCP/PXE on the site LAN, config fetch from the controller, then claim and follow-on OpenTofu/Ansible from Core.

When DHCP controls appear in the UI, treat enablement as a controlled network event. An unintended DHCP server can conflict with existing address assignment on a shared segment. Use a change window and confirm you own the broadcast domain first.

Recommended flow

  1. Prepare baseline templates (and Image Nest media if you need Windows/Linux install artifacts).
  2. Review pending/discovered devices in Fleet Incoming before claim.
  3. Apply provisioning and validate post-state.
  4. Record deviations and update templates.
Where DHCP/PXE runs: site LAN DHCP and /ztp/fetch live on the Hayabusa Controller. Hayabusa Core orchestrates claim, GitOps, and OpenTofu/Ansible after LAN Approve — it does not bind customer-LAN DHCP on the hub. With a controller branch selected, the UI rewrites ZTP/DHCP API calls to /api/my-controller/ztp/* (status, config, interfaces, leases, start/stop, pending-devices).

7.1) Image Nest — Windows & Linux install media

Image Nest is a Controller feature (open it from the connected controller UI at /image-nest). You bring a legal Windows or Linux ISO; Hayabusa never redistributes Microsoft media. Nest banks dated artifacts and publishes hydrate placeholders such as <<IMAGE_NEST:latest-win11-stripped>> for OpenTofu/Ansible on LAN Approve.

Deploy targets on the Image Nest page

Works for Windows and Linux ISOs. Pick a target first — the banked file type changes with the target:

TargetWindows outputLinux outputUse for
Bare metal Bootable ISO Bootable ISO ZTP / PXE / USB physical install
Hypervisor media install.wim (+ empty qcow2 stub) Banked ISO OpenTofu / KVM attach as install media
Bootable disk virt-install → bootable qcow2 (needs KVM tools on controller) ISO + qcow2 stub Proxmox / KVM guest disk
Hyper-V Bootable convert → VHDX ISO + empty VHDX stub Hyper-V guests
VMware Bootable convert → VMDK ISO + empty VMDK stub VMware guests

Buttons and actions

  • Strip & bank / Full bank — bake for the selected deploy target (optional package strip).
  • Publish ISO as-is — bank the uploaded medium for bare metal without a strip bake.
  • Guest initial config — hostname, admin user, password, disk size for Windows Autounattend when baking Bootable / Hyper-V / VMware disks.
  • → VHDX / → VMDK on a bank row — convert an existing banked disk with qemu-img.
  • Hayabusa hand-off — write tfvars for Core; the controller never runs OpenTofu.

How this ties to OpenTofu / ZTP

  1. Upload ISO on the controller Image Nest page.
  2. Choose target → Strip/Full bank (or Publish ISO as-is).
  3. In OpenTofu/Ansible commit placeholders only, e.g. source_image = "<<IMAGE_NEST:latest-win11-bootable>>".
  4. Queue the job from Hayabusa; on LAN Approve the controller hydrates nest refs (same pattern as secrets).
  5. Core applies OpenTofu/Ansible with resolved paths.
Empty disk stubs are not a runnable OS. A blank qcow2/VHDX/VMDK is a container waiting for install. A bootable Windows qcow2 requires virt-install (or a separately prepared disk). Linux disk targets today bank the ISO plus an empty platform-native stub unless you convert a real disk later.
Proven lab path: bootable Win11 qcow2 hydrated from the controller into Proxmox at OpenTofu create (no ISO staged on Proxmox first). See controller Image Nest bank aliases such as latest-win11-hypervisor / latest-win11-bootable.

8) Security and Pentesting

Security workflows in Hayabusa support authorized assessment and risk discovery — not unscoped scanning of networks you do not own. Pair every scan or SECops playbook with scope control, approval, and a remediation path.

What lives where

  • Fleet / DevOps workspace — SECops Ansible trees and related playbooks under your tenant workspace (often with packaged defaults that GitOps does not overwrite).
  • Controller — site-local secrets and LAN Approve for jobs that need vault material or Image Nest media.
  • Observability / alerts — evidence after changes; optional AI suggest-from-alert paths when enabled.

Responsible workflow

  1. Define scope and written approval (systems, CIDRs, time window).
  2. Run low-impact reconnaissance first.
  3. Escalate to active checks only where authorized.
  4. Convert findings to tracked remediation tasks; re-test after fixes.
Legal boundary: only assess systems you own or are explicitly authorized to test. Unauthorized scanning can be unlawful and can disrupt production.

9) Integrations

Integrations connect Hayabusa to outside services and workflows — chat login providers, Git (GitOps), observability backends, Incoming, vendor ZTP, and optional AI providers. Every integration should have an owner, expected behavior, and a rollback plan.

Integration Builder ↔ controller: webhooks, custom builder apps, and automation jobs save on Core and sync to the selected controller with POST /api/my-controller/integrations/sync (pull then push of integrations.bundle.*). The Integrations UI Sync button uses that path; builder app upserts also best-effort push so the site appliance keeps the same bundle.

Common integration families

  • Identity — Google, Slack, Discord, Microsoft sign-in (Core).

10) AI and User Keys

Hayabusa supports user-scoped AI keys for assistant functionality. Store keys in your controller and use the SECRET NAMES on Hayabusa core. Never put secrets on Hayabusa core.

  • Use least-privilege keys where possible.
  • Avoid sending secrets in prompts.
  • Validate AI outputs before operational use.

What the embedded AI helper now understands

The floating Hayabusa AI Assistant is aware of the main operating surfaces and should guide users to the correct page, tab, and safety boundary. It can explain Workstations map views, the Ansible/OpenTofu console and Graphical View, tenant-scoped openPLC/ directories, Apache Incoming consoles, Observability, SCADA/PLC workflows, ROS2 robotics tooling, ZTP/controller roles, and Image Nest deploy targets.

Recommended prompts

  • "Show me how to switch from my personal observability view to my organization Grafana view."
  • "How do I upload an OpenPLC project folder into the Graphical View?"
  • "Which PLC protocols can I read or write from the PLC Viewer, and what safety checks apply?"
  • "Where do I find the Robotics Viewer and what ROS2 tools are installed?"
AI safety expectation: the helper can explain and draft steps, but operators must verify identity scope, organization scope, target host/controller, and authorization before running commands or writing PLC values.

11) Grafana, Prometheus, and InfluxDB

Hayabusa exposes the observability stack through authenticated subpath proxies: /grafana/, /prometheus/, /influxdb/, and the Hayabusa dashboard at /observability. The goal is to keep Grafana, Prometheus, and InfluxDB usable without asking operators to leave Hayabusa or bypass tenant controls.

How the stack is separated

  • User scope: personal metrics, dashboards, datasources, and retention settings are associated with the signed-in user context.
  • Organization scope: organization metrics and dashboards are associated with the selected organization context and require the user to be a member of that organization.
  • Grafana organization separation: Hayabusa provisions or selects Grafana organizations and passes the appropriate auth proxy headers so users land in the expected Grafana org.
  • Prometheus query separation: Hayabusa rewrites tenant-aware PromQL queries where appropriate so metric results are filtered to the selected user or organization scope.
  • InfluxDB bucket separation: InfluxDB bucket access and retention are tied to the tenant bucket model used by Hayabusa's observability bootstrap logic.

Using the Observability page

  1. Open Observability from the top navigation or use the Observability map view on Workstations.
  2. Confirm whether the page is in personal or organization context. If you are a member of organizations, use the scope selector to switch deliberately.
  3. Use the Grafana panel for dashboards, Prometheus for metric queries and targets, and InfluxDB for time-series bucket inspection where enabled.
  4. Check the status cards before troubleshooting a dashboard. A dashboard issue may be caused by a downstream service, proxy path, datasource, or tenant scope mismatch.

Grafana

Grafana is used for dashboarding, alert visualization, datasource exploration, and organization-scoped views. Hayabusa proxies Grafana under /grafana/ and adjusts subpath behavior so assets, API calls, avatars, and dashboard links resolve under Hayabusa instead of the Grafana container root.

  • Use Grafana dashboards for high-level operational views.
  • Use Explore for ad hoc metric questions.
  • Use the Hayabusa scope selector before opening Grafana when you need personal vs organization separation.
  • If a panel looks empty, confirm datasource scope, time range, and tenant labels before assuming data loss.

Prometheus

Prometheus stores and queries operational metrics. Hayabusa proxies Prometheus under /prometheus/ and supports tenant-aware query behavior for user and organization separation. Prometheus is best for instant/range queries, target health, rule evaluation, and low-level metric debugging.

  • Use the Prometheus query UI when you need raw metric evidence.
  • Use target health to distinguish scrape failures from dashboard problems.
  • Use scalar queries such as time() normally; Hayabusa avoids turning scalar expressions into invalid tenant-filtered expressions.

InfluxDB

InfluxDB is used for time-series buckets and data that fits the Influx model. Hayabusa proxies InfluxDB under /influxdb/, rewrites root-relative UI paths for static assets, and exposes health/setup calls through the Hayabusa auth boundary.

  • Use InfluxDB for bucket-level inspection and time-series workflows configured by operators.
  • Use tenant bucket names and retention settings to understand where data lives.
  • If the Influx UI appears to onboard unexpectedly, verify the configured admin token and bucket/bootstrap state.

Retention and administration

Retention can be controlled globally by administrators and per allowed tenant context. Admin retention updates are exposed on the Administration page and through /api/admin/observability/retention. User/org observability settings are exposed through /api/observability/retention.

Troubleshooting observability

  • Static asset 404s: verify requests include the Hayabusa subpath such as /grafana/, /prometheus/, or /influxdb/.
  • Empty Grafana dashboards: confirm tenant scope, datasource provisioning, and the dashboard time range.
  • Prometheus query errors: check the raw PromQL and whether the query is tenant-label compatible.
  • Influx setup/onboarding screens: confirm PEREGRINE_INFLUX_ADMIN_TOKEN, bucket existence, and service health.
  • Live websocket warnings: confirm whether the upstream feature is enabled and whether the browser request is being made through the correct Hayabusa path.

12) SCADA, PLC, ROS2, and NVIDIA Isaac

Hayabusa now includes industrial-control and robotics surfaces for lab, emulator, and authorized real-system workflows. These tools are powerful and must be operated with explicit authorization and correct target scope.

SCADA viewer

The SCADA Workstations view embeds FUXA, an open-source browser-based SCADA/HMI screen builder. Use it when the operator task is about controller status, process visibility, protocol targeting, or SCADA-style context rather than general host infrastructure.

  • Start in SCADA when working with controllers, plants, cells, lines, or emulator labs.
  • Use FUXA for HMI screens, alarms, process visualization, and industrial-protocol presentation.
  • Use it together with PLC Viewer for protocol-specific reads/writes.
  • Use Observability alongside SCADA to correlate controller actions with metrics and host telemetry.

FUXA is installed inside the Hayabusa application container and started by /peregrine/start-server.sh when /opt/fuxa/server/main.js is present. It listens internally on FUXA_PORT (default 1881) and stores runtime data under PEREGRINE_FUXA_DATA_DIR (default /var/lib/peregrine/fuxa). Hayabusa exposes the embedded browser UI through the same-origin /fuxa/ proxy and exposes /api/scada/status so the SCADA view can show whether FUXA is reachable and where the browser-facing service is located. Protect the FUXA URL with network controls and FUXA/Hayabusa access policy; do not expose unrestricted write-capable SCADA screens to untrusted networks.

PLC Viewer

The PLC Viewer is available from the tabbed console surfaces on Fleet and Workstations. It supports unified PLC reads and controlled writes through Hayabusa APIs. Current protocol families include Modbus TCP, OPC UA, Siemens S7, Allen-Bradley CIP, Beckhoff ADS, Mitsubishi MC, and Omron FINS UDP, depending on installed driver availability.

Reading PLC data

  1. Open Fleet or Workstations and select the relevant host/controller context.
  2. Open the console window and select PLC Viewer.
  3. Select the protocol and enter the target host, port, address, register/tag/symbol, unit/device ID, and count/type fields required by that protocol.
  4. Click Read PLC and review the JSON result.

Writing PLC data

PLC writes are supported but guarded. The server requires the global plc_writes_enabled operator safety flag, and the user must explicitly confirm authorization in the UI for each write request. Write attempts are audited whether they succeed or fail.

  1. Confirm you own or are approved to control the target controller or emulator.
  2. Confirm admin policy allows PLC writes. In development images this can be controlled with PEREGRINE_PLC_WRITES_ENABLED and persisted safety policy.
  3. Enter the write value(s), function code/tag/symbol, and protocol-specific addressing.
  4. Check I am authorized to write to this PLC.
  5. Click Write PLC and verify the result out of band before continuing.
PLC write safety: a write can change real-world state. Use writes only on systems you own, systems you are approved to control, or emulated lab controllers. Start with reads, use minimal writes, and preserve an emergency stop/rollback path.

Programming and OpenPLC

OpenPLC Editor and MatIEC support controller-program development workflows. Keep source files under the tenant-scoped openPLC/ workspace directory, edit them in Graphical View or console, and use OpenPLC tooling from the container when you need project editing or code generation.

Robotics Viewer and ROS2

The Robotics Viewer Workstations view is the robotics-oriented operating surface. ROS2 Humble tooling is installed in the Hayabusa container, including ros2, rosdep, colcon, and base ROS packages.   • Use ros2 node list to identify active nodes when attached to a ROS2-enabled environment.

MAVLink/MAVSDK connection workflow

The Robotics Viewer includes a MAVLink endpoint form for real telemetry connections. Hayabusa stores the endpoint per user or organization scope, starts a tenant-scoped MAVLink helper process, and reads live heartbeat, position, attitude, GPS, battery, status text, command acknowledgements, parameters, mission state, and richer flight data through PyMAVLink.   5. Watch the live telemetry panel for heartbeat, position, battery, attitude, GPS, status text, and command messages.

Hayabusa Native GCS, ADS-B, and Remote ID

The Robotics Viewer is Hayabusa's native ground-control station for licensed-safe drone operations without bundling Qt/QGroundControl or GPL Mission Planner. It includes a drone world map for ownship MAVLink position, ADS-B aircraft decoded by dump1090, and Remote ID drone tracks ingested from an OpenDroneID/DroneScanner-style bridge. It is designed for tenant-scoped telemetry, operator safety gates, audit logs, and browser-native mission/control workflows.

  • Native flight deck: Hayabusa supports flight mode changes, parameter fetch/edit, command ACK display, mission download, mission clear, guarded goto, and guarded waypoint mission upload through the active MAVLink helper.
  • Optional QGroundControl / Mission Planner: Hayabusa can still store tenant-scoped launcher URLs for external or Incoming-hosted ground-station desktops. These are optional compatibility tools, not bundled dependencies. QGroundControl requires Qt licensing care; Mission Planner is GPLv3 and should remain external/separate.
  • dump1090 / RTL-SDR: the container includes rtl-sdr and dump1090-mutability where installed. Hayabusa can start/stop a tenant-scoped dump1090 background receiver bound to loopback, reads its aircraft JSON, and plots decoded ADS-B aircraft.
  • OpenDroneID / DroneScanner / OpenSpyglass-style Remote ID: Hayabusa exposes a tenant-scoped ingest endpoint for decoded Remote ID tracks. A local bridge process can listen on an Alfa Wi-Fi card or Bluetooth receiver, decode Remote ID packets, and POST normalized track JSON into Hayabusa.
  • Safety controls: writes such as mode changes, parameter edits, mission clear, goto, arm/takeoff/land/RTL, and mission upload require explicit authorization and confirmation text.
  • Multi-drone fleet: save multiple vehicles (id, label, MAVLink endpoint, camera URL) per tenant with /api/robotics/fleet. Each vehicle gets its own MAVLink helper session. Pass vehicle_id on connect, commands, missions, and native actions, or choose the active vehicle in the Robotics Viewer fleet panel.
  • Gamepad / joystick: the browser Gamepad API sends MAVLink MANUAL_CONTROL to the active vehicle after guarded confirmation text enable joystick. Use only on authorized aircraft in a suitable manual flight mode with a visual observer and emergency recovery plan.
  • Cameras: configure per-vehicle camera_url values (HTTP/HTTPS MJPEG, RTSP, HLS, or embed). Hayabusa proxies MJPEG/RTSP through /api/robotics/camera/<vehicle_id>/stream for same-origin viewing. Do not embed credentials in camera URLs.
Airspace and radio warning: ADS-B and Remote ID receivers are passive situational-awareness tools. Do not rely on them as the only collision-avoidance source. Follow local aviation, RF, privacy, and Remote ID laws, and use only hardware you are authorized to operate.
Joystick safety: gamepad input can move a real aircraft immediately. Arm joystick control only after verifying the correct active vehicle, suitable flight mode, geofence/RTL policy, and local regulations. Disarm when not actively flying.

15) Administration

Administration controls global platform behavior — safety toggles, identity posture, and operational visibility. Treat admin actions as reversible control-plane operations with a defined owner and verification signal.

Before changing global toggles, define: expected blast radius, rollback condition, and how you will know it worked (login success rate, tunnel health, host freshness, or scrape health).

Subsections in this category

  • 15.1 Safety Controls — sign-in, Headscale, bans/allowlists, throttling.
  • 15.2 Manual Identity Controls — local/fallback auth paths.
  • 15.3 Logs and Operational Visibility — audit and accountability.
Break-glass: always preserve at least one recovery admin path before disabling sign-ins or overlay controls during an incident.

15.1) Safety Controls

Safety controls exist to protect platform integrity during incidents, abuse events, and maintenance windows.

What each control does

  • Sign-in toggle: temporarily blocks new sign-ins (preserves emergency paths configured by policy).
  • Headscale toggle: disables/reenables overlay control behavior during network investigations.
  • Bandwidth throttling controls: operator-level constraints for controlled behavior.
  • Bans: prevent specific identities from accessing the platform.
  • Allowlists: define explicit identities permitted under tighter posture.

How to operate safety controls

  1. Define incident objective first (containment, recovery, investigation).
  2. Apply minimum required control change.
  3. Document who changed what and why.
  4. Monitor service behavior.
  5. Revert controls when safe and record closure notes.
Critical: never apply broad safety changes without communicating impact to operators and users. Always preserve at least one break-glass admin recovery path.

15.2) Manual Identity Controls

Manual identity tooling supports controlled local auth workflows and admin fallback when federated providers (Google, Slack, Discord, Microsoft) are unavailable or policy requires an emergency path.

When to use it

  • Provider outage or misconfiguration blocks normal sign-in.
  • Policy requires a temporary local account for a controlled maintenance window.
  • Incident response needs a known-good admin identity independent of IdP health.

Recommended usage

  • Enable only when required by policy or incident response.
  • Use strong credentials; rotate after high-risk windows.
  • Disable fallback paths when no longer needed; record who enabled them and why.
  • Prefer federated login for day-to-day users to reduce password custody on Core.
Do not leave emergency local auth enabled permanently in production without compensating controls and review.

15.3) Logs and Operational Visibility

Admin-level visibility includes audit history, safety actions, and network-related event logs. Use logs for accountability and root-cause analysis — not only for “when something breaks.”

Minimum logging discipline

  • Record actor, action, target, and reason for safety and identity changes.
  • Correlate changes with incident timelines (and with Grafana/Prometheus when metrics moved).
  • Store enough context for post-incident review; avoid logging secrets or raw credentials.
  • Review PLC write audits and robotics command audits after any guarded write session.

16) Architecture and Runtime

Hayabusa Core runs as a containerized application (Flask/gunicorn in typical deploys) composed of templates, static assets, and route handlers. Controllers are separate site appliances (API, DHCP/PXE edge, secrets, Image Nest, LAN Approve). Operational behavior is always code + persisted state.

When debugging, verify both: “Is the code path deployed?” and “Is runtime state what we think it is?” Map telemetry is sampled asynchronously and rendered as periodic snapshots — freshness and sampler heartbeat distinguish a quiet network from a stale pipeline.

Core vs Controller (one diagram in words)

  • Core — public/brain: identity, Fleet/Workstations, GitOps agent, OpenTofu/Ansible orchestration, observability proxies.
  • Controller — site edge: dnsmasq DHCP/PXE, /ztp/fetch, secrets vault, Image Nest, job hydrate on LAN Approve.
  • Git — optional Advanced source of truth; not the LAN boot pipe.
Longer architecture write-ups live in the repo under docs/ZTP-AND-CONTROLLER-ARCHITECTURE.md and docs/IMAGE-NEST-AND-WINDOWS-IMAGES.md.

17) Persistence and State

Hayabusa persists key operational state to disk volumes: identity linkage, tenant network allocations, safety policy, user-scoped encrypted materials, workspaces, and controller data (leases, nest bank, vault).

Know which controls survive restarts and which are session-derived. Some diagnostics (immediate baseline compare, transient path highlights) are intentionally runtime-scoped for speed. If compliance needs durable history, add explicit storage policy rather than assuming every visualization is persisted.

Examples

  • Persists — org memberships, tenant CIDRs, Image Nest bank on the controller volume, Grafana orgs/datasources (as provisioned).
  • Session / ephemeral — browser session cookies, some map overlays, in-memory baselines.

18) Common Runbooks

Runbook: New operator onboarding

  1. Verify identity and role assignment (Profile + org selector).
  2. Walk Workstations → Fleet → Networking in read-only mode (§3–§6).
  3. Execute one approved low-risk action (refresh, inspect, AI draft review).

Runbook: Contain suspicious account activity

  1. Apply account ban/allowlist controls (Admin safety).
  2. If needed, disable new sign-ins temporarily — keep break-glass admin.
  3. Review audit and network logs.
  4. Document and restore normal posture once resolved.

Runbook: Tenant subnet issue

  1. Review tenant allocation records (§6.3).
  2. Validate overlap constraints and expected ownership.
  3. Apply corrected assignment with validation.
  4. Confirm connectivity and overlay/IPsec behavior.

Runbook: Overlay tunnel slow but not down

  1. Open Workstations Overlay and inspect link health color/state.
  2. Open Underlay and confirm whether adjacent links show elevated jitter/loss.
  3. Run Path Trace between affected source/destination IPs.
  4. Use telemetry drawer top talkers to identify likely traffic contributors.
  5. Apply smallest corrective action first (policy, route, selector, or rate control), then re-verify health trend.

Runbook: Bank Windows/Linux media then create a VM

  1. On the controller, open Image Nest (/image-nest) and upload a legal ISO (§7.1).
  2. Choose deploy target (bare metal / hypervisor media / bootable / Hyper-V / VMware) and Strip or Full bank.
  3. Commit OpenTofu with <<IMAGE_NEST:…>> placeholders only.
  4. Queue from Hayabusa; LAN Approve on the controller; apply on Core.
  5. Verify guest boot; remember empty disk stubs are not a runnable OS.

19) Troubleshooting

Symptom: Page looks outdated or broken

  • Hard refresh browser assets (Ctrl/Cmd+Shift+R).
  • Verify auth session and route existence.
  • Check whether Core was restarted after template/image changes.

Symptom: Host list or network status inconsistent

  • Refresh discovery and overlay states.
  • Validate safety toggles and Headscale/WireGuard posture.
  • Inspect recent admin actions in logs.
  • Confirm organization selector matches the inventory you expect.

Symptom: Access unexpectedly denied

  • Check role/scope context (Profile + org).
  • Verify active bans/allowlist policy.
  • Confirm admin identity matching policy inputs.

Symptom: Link health appears severe but throughput is high

  • Check whether loss or jitter thresholds are tripping critical-state logic.
  • Confirm telemetry freshness and sampler heartbeat before acting.
  • Correlate with top-talker behavior and path trace results.
  • Treat this as quality degradation, not capacity success.

Symptom: OpenTofu/Ansible cannot find an image or secret

  • Confirm placeholders are still in git (not real paths/passwords).
  • Confirm the job was LAN Approved on the controller that holds the vault/Nest bank.
  • Check Image Nest registry aliases (§7.1) and secret names on the controller.

Symptom: /docs section missing after an update

  • Hard refresh; open the section hash directly (e.g. /docs#image-nest).
  • Confirm Core image/template includes the latest docs.html (DEV_SYNC or rebuild).

20) Best Practices

  • Separate hosts (using Opentofu) and their data (Ansible) to facilitate easy recreation, replication, and to protect against user error.
  • Test before deploying Consider testing scripts before deploying. You can always put a controller in a test environment to test on low-risk hosts first.
  • Make scope explicit before every change (user vs org, site vs lab).
  • Prefer staged rollouts over broad immediate actions; use OpenTofu plans and Ansible check modes where available.
  • Least privilege for accounts, org roles, AI keys, and all credentials.
  • Placeholders only in git — prefer {{ hayabusa_secret:KEY }} or <<SECRET:KEY>> (plus <<IMAGE_NEST:…>>); hydrate on LAN Approve.
  • Auditability by default — who changed what, when, and why.
  • Environment-specific runbooks — this manual is general; your CIDRs, change windows, and owners are local.
  • Use AI to draft, humans to approve — especially for OpenTofu, Ansible, PLC, and MAVLink commands.
  • Empty Nest stubs are not OS images — know ISO vs WIM vs bootable disk vs VHDX/VMDK before attaching media.

21) FAQ

Does this page include API route listings?

No. This page is operational and conceptual. Use /api for route lists and endpoint details.

Can new users become productive from this guide?

Yes — start with §3 onboarding, then Ansible/OpenTofu (§4.2) and Fleet (§5). Teams should still keep local runbooks for environment-specific standards.

What is the fastest way to avoid mistakes?

Verify identity scope and target scope (org, host, CIDR, PLC/drone) before executing changes. Prefer dry-runs and LAN Approve for secrets/media.

Do I need a Hayabusa Controller?

Core alone supports sign-in, maps, and crafting. A controller on the site LAN is required for DHCP/PXE ZTP, Image Nest banking, and LAN Approve hydration of secrets and nest refs.

OpenTofu vs Ansible — which do I use?

OpenTofu when the resource must converge to a declared state (often VMs/cloud/objects). Ansible when you need procedural configuration with softer failure semantics. Many stacks use both.

Where do Windows/Linux ISOs get prepared?

On the controller Image Nest page (/image-nest). See §7.1 for deploy targets (bare metal, hypervisor media, bootable disk, Hyper-V, VMware).

Why is my Grafana dashboard empty?

Confirm personal vs organization scope, datasource provisioning, time range, and tenant labels — then check Prometheus targets. See §11.

How do I contact Tracedroute support?

Two options: open an in-app support ticket on Service Orders, or email support@tracedroute.net.

22) Derivatives

In Hayabusa, a derivative is any reusable operational unit that originates from core capabilities without becoming a separate product. Derivatives reuse Hayabusa’s identity, network, governance, and operational model.

Examples

  • Workspace templates and runbook variants (Ansible/OpenTofu packs).
  • Policy overlays (safety, allowlists, tenant conventions).
  • Integration adapters (GitOps layouts, vendor ZTP packs).
  • Tenant-specific workflow packs and deployment conventions.
  • Image Nest bake recipes and Autounattend profiles reused across sites.

How to manage derivatives well

  • Keep each derivative versioned, scoped (user/org/site), and owned.
  • Document where it diverges from base platform expectations.
  • Prefer placeholders for secrets and Image Nest refs so packs stay portable.
  • Before adoption, verify security assumptions, identity scope, network boundaries, and rollback strategy.
Rule of thumb: if removing Hayabusa identity/network/governance would make the pack useless, it is a derivative — not a fork of Hayabusa itself.
